An alert fires late, or a software update changes what a screen shows, and a nurse mentions it at handover. Everyone assumes someone else has reported it. In Singapore, the Health Sciences Authority (HSA) sets out different roles for companies and for healthcare professionals, and knowing them helps a ward decide what to write down and whom to tell.

## Who reports

HSA’s [medical device adverse events page](https://www.hsa.gov.sg/medical-devices/adverse-events) says all companies dealing in medical devices, including importers, manufacturers, suppliers and registrants, are required to report adverse events of their products. For dealers it gives time limits, for example 48 hours for a serious threat to public health and 10 calendar days for a death or serious deterioration in health.

Healthcare professionals are **encouraged** to report. HSA’s [guide for healthcare professionals](https://www.hsa.gov.sg/adverse-events/for-healthcare-professionals/) encourages reporting serious adverse events related to the use of health products as soon as you are aware of them.

Software matters here too. On [field safety corrective actions](https://www.hsa.gov.sg/medical-devices/advertisement-post-market-surveillance-and-safety-monitoring/field-safety-corrective-action/), HSA notes that these actions may include recalls, product modifications, software updates, relabelling or updated instructions for use.

Whether a given AI tool is a medical device at all depends on its intended use, so ask your vendor and regulatory contact. Software that is not a device may need a different route inside the hospital.

## What to write down at the bedside

HSA’s guide asks for device details such as brand name, device type, model or catalogue number, serial, lot or batch number, what went wrong and what happened to the patient. A ward sheet can capture the same things. The following is our suggestion.

1. Date, time and location
2. Device or software name, and version if visible on screen
3. Identifier from the label (a photo of the label can help, with no patient information in the picture)
4. What was expected and what happened
5. What staff did, and who was told

## Agree the path before it is needed

- **Who in the hospital receives the ward sheet?** Name a role and a deputy.
- **Who contacts the supplier**, so that the dealer learns of it promptly? Dealer time limits run from when their personnel are made aware.
- **Who submits a report to HSA** if the hospital decides to?
- **Who reads vendor notices** about software updates and passes them to the wards?

## Try it this week

Pick one device that uses software. Ask two shifts what they would do if it behaved oddly, then check whether the supplier contact on the wall is current.

## Key takeaways

- Reporting is mandatory for device dealers and encouraged for healthcare professionals.
- Software updates can be part of a field safety corrective action.
- A short ward sheet with device identifiers makes follow-up easier.
- This is general information, not legal advice. Check current HSA pages and your own policy.
