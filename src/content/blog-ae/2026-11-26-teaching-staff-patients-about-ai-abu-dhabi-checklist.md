A new AI tool usually arrives with a vendor demonstration for the first group of users. Then the rota turns over, new staff join, and a patient asks at the bedside, “Is a computer deciding this?” If nobody was given the job of explaining, each ward improvises its own answer. Abu Dhabi’s AI policy puts that job on the organisations that use AI.

## What the policy says

The Department of Health’s [Policy on Use of AI in the Healthcare Sector](https://www.doh.gov.ae/-/media/E9C1470A575146B18015DEBE57E47F8D.ashx) (Policy/AI/0.9, version 0.9, published 30 April 2018) applies, among others, to DoH-licensed healthcare providers. Section 7.2 sets duties for healthcare end-users other than patients. Among them:

- have clear governance on the use of AI (7.2.1);
- make clear where AI enters the process, what the physician or other practitioner does, what the AI does, and the boundaries between them (7.2.2);
- ensure education on AI and its effective use for all those involved, including patients (7.2.4);
- conduct regular audits of AI functionality, reporting to DoH as needed (7.2.5);
- educate end-users, including patients, on AI and its effective use (7.2.8).

Section 9 also asks end-users other than patients and insurers to report known or suspected AI incidents or deficiencies to DoH and other regulatory bodies.

The version we read is numbered 0.9 and dates from 2018. Ask your compliance team whether later DoH documents add to it or change it. This article covers Abu Dhabi; Dubai has its own policy.

## A one-page training plan

The policy does not prescribe a format. The following is our suggestion.

1. **One row per AI tool.** Name it, and write in one sentence what it does.
2. **Who needs to know.** Nurses, front desk, medical records, doctors, patients.
3. **What each group needs.** Staff: what the output means, what to do when it looks wrong, whom to call. Patients: that a tool is used, and who makes the decision.
4. **When.** Before first use, at induction, and after a major software update.
5. **Who owns it.** A named role, with a deputy.

## A script for the bedside

Staff often want words they can actually say. Draft two or three plain sentences with your clinical and patient-experience leads, in the languages your patients use. For example: “This software helps us check for certain risks. It does not make the decision; your care team does. Please ask us if you have questions.” Use it only if it is true for that tool: the second sentence must match how the tool really works.

## Try it this week

Pick one tool in use. Ask three people on different shifts two questions: what does it do, and what would you do if you disagreed with it? Where answers differ, start the training there.

## Key takeaways

- Abu Dhabi’s AI policy asks provider organisations to educate those involved, including patients.
- It also asks for clear boundaries between the AI’s role and the practitioner’s.
- A one-page plan per tool, with a named owner, is a practical start.
- This is general information, not legal advice. Confirm current requirements with your compliance team.
