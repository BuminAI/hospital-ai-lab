Most AI planning in hospitals starts with features, cost, and security. A question that often comes later is what to tell patients. A Pew Research Center survey published August 25, 2026 gives useful data on what patients expect. This post summarizes it for administrators and nursing leaders. It is general information, not legal advice.

## What the survey found

Pew [surveyed 3,488 U.S. adults](https://www.pewresearch.org/short-reads/2026/08/25/americans-want-transparency-when-ai-is-used-in-their-healthcare/) from June 22 to 28, 2026. Overall, 72% said it is extremely or very important that a doctor or other provider tells them if AI is being used in their care.

Asked task by task, the share saying patients should be told was:

- Analyze medical scans: 81%
- Make a diagnosis: 81%
- Explain lab results: 80%
- Take notes during an appointment: 72%
- Order prescription refills from a pharmacy: 64%
- Schedule an appointment: 56%

Also, 63% would like more say over whether AI is used in their care, while 21% are comfortable with the say they have now. Only 16% said their provider has used AI in their care; 37% said no and 46% were not sure.

## What stands out for hospital teams

Clinical uses drew the strongest demand for disclosure, as you might expect. But note-taking, which is often treated as a back-office task, still reached 72%. That matters for teams rolling out AI documentation tools, where nurses and clinicians are the people patients will ask. And nearly half of respondents did not know whether AI had been part of their care at all. The survey measures attitudes; it does not say what any law requires.

## Three things to prepare before a rollout

These are our general suggestions, not survey findings. Check your own approach with compliance, legal, and patient experience staff.

1. **Make an inventory of AI uses.** List where AI touches care and operations, such as imaging support, documentation drafts, and scheduling. It is hard to explain what the organization itself has not mapped.
2. **Decide who explains, and when.** For documentation tools used at the bedside or in clinic, agree whether the explanation comes from the clinician, the nurse, or written materials, and use consistent wording.
3. **Prepare answers to likely questions.** Patients may ask who makes the final decision or how their data is used. Have the responsible teams approve those answers ahead of time so front-line staff are not improvising.

## Key takeaways

- In a June 2026 Pew survey of 3,488 U.S. adults, 72% said being told about AI use is extremely or very important.
- 81% want to be told when AI analyzes scans or helps diagnose; 72% when it takes notes at a visit.
- 46% were not sure whether their provider had used AI at all.
- Hospitals can prepare an AI use inventory, a plan for who explains it, and approved answers to common questions.
