Copying a whole note into an AI tool is faster than picking out the three lines the task needs. For summaries, appeal letters, or staff schedules built from records, that habit can send far more patient information than necessary. The HIPAA Privacy Rule has a standard that speaks to this, and it is a useful starting point for a unit-level rule.

## What the rule says

Under [45 CFR 164.502(b)](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-45/section-164.502), a covered entity or business associate must make reasonable efforts to limit protected health information to the minimum necessary for the intended purpose of a use, disclosure, or request.

There are exceptions. Among them, the standard does not apply to disclosures to or requests by a health care provider for treatment, to uses or disclosures made to the individual, or to those made under the individual's authorization.

[45 CFR 164.514(d)](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-45/section-164.514) adds how this works in practice:

- A covered entity identifies which people or classes of people in its workforce need access, and to which categories of information.
- For routine, recurring disclosures, it has policies and procedures that limit what is sent.
- An entire medical record is not used, disclosed, or requested unless the entire record is specifically justified as reasonably necessary.

The rule does not mention AI. Whether a particular AI use is permitted at all, including whether a business associate agreement is needed, is a separate question. Take that one to your privacy officer before any real patient data goes in.

## A one-page check for each AI task

The steps below are our suggestions, not regulatory text.

1. **Name the task.** "Draft a discharge teaching handout" is a task. "Help with this patient" is not.
2. **List the fields the task needs.** A teaching handout may need the topic and reading level, and no identifiers at all.
3. **Name who may do it.** Match the role to the access your policy already gives it.
4. **Start from approved tools only.** Say which tools are approved, and say plainly that public consumer chatbots are not, if that is your policy.
5. **Write down the "never" list.** Full chart exports, lists of patients, and screenshots of the record are common candidates.

## Try it this week

Pick one unit and one task that staff already do with AI, or want to. Ask two nurses and one office staff member to write down what they would paste. Compare the answers. Where one person pastes the whole note and another pastes two sentences, you have found the line your guidance needs to draw.

Make it easy to ask. A named contact and a short answer time will do more than a long policy that nobody opens. Review the list again when the tool, its contract, or the task changes.

## Key takeaways

- The minimum necessary standard asks for reasonable efforts to limit information to what the purpose needs.
- It has exceptions, including provider-to-provider treatment disclosures and requests.
- It does not by itself approve an AI tool; check permission and agreements separately.
- Writing the needed fields per task is something a unit can do this week.

This is general information, not legal advice. Confirm how the rule applies to your organization with your privacy or compliance officer.
