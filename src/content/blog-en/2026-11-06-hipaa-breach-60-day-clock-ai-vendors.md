When an AI vendor holds patient data for you and something goes wrong, the question that matters most in the first week is not technical. It is: when did the clock start? HIPAA's breach notification rules answer that, and the answer affects what you should ask for in a vendor contract.

## What counts as a breach

Under 45 CFR 164.402, a breach is an acquisition, access, use or disclosure of protected health information that the Privacy Rule does not permit and that compromises its security or privacy. Such an event is presumed to be a breach unless the covered entity or business associate can show a low probability that the information was compromised. That showing must rest on a risk assessment of at least four factors: the nature and extent of the information, who used it or received it, whether it was actually acquired or viewed, and how far the risk has been reduced. Three narrow exclusions are listed in the definition.

## The clock

A hospital must notify each affected individual without unreasonable delay and no later than 60 calendar days after it discovers the breach (§ 164.404(b)). A breach is treated as discovered on the first day it is known, or would have been known with reasonable diligence, to any workforce member or agent of the hospital other than the person who committed it.

Two other notices run on the same 60-day outer limit or close to it. For a breach affecting more than 500 residents of a state, the hospital must notify prominent media outlets serving that state (§ 164.406). For 500 or more individuals, HHS must be notified at the same time as the individuals; for fewer than 500, the hospital keeps a log and reports to HHS within 60 days after the end of the calendar year (§ 164.408).

## Where the vendor comes in

A business associate must notify the hospital of a breach without unreasonable delay and no later than 60 calendar days after it discovers it (§ 164.410). The text sets that same outer limit for the vendor. It also says that a breach is treated as discovered by the business associate when known to its employees, officers or agents.

The rules do not say that the hospital's own clock waits for the vendor's notice. The hospital's discovery standard covers its agents. Whether a vendor counts as an agent depends on the federal common law of agency, and that is a question for counsel. The practical point is simple: if a vendor uses most of its 60 days before telling you, you may have little time left.

## What to put in the contract

- A vendor notice period much shorter than 60 days, counted from the vendor's own discovery.
- A named 24-hour contact and a duty to share the facts you need for the patient notice.
- A list of subcontractors that touch the data, since a subcontractor's breach reaches you through the vendor.

These are recommendations, not legal requirements; the rule sets only the outer limit.

## Key takeaways

- A breach is presumed unless a four-factor risk assessment shows a low probability of compromise (45 CFR 164.402).
- Patients must be notified no later than 60 calendar days after discovery; HHS and media notices follow their own thresholds (§§ 164.404–164.408).
- A business associate must notify the hospital within 60 days at most (§ 164.410).
- Ask for a much shorter vendor notice period and a 24-hour contact in the contract.
