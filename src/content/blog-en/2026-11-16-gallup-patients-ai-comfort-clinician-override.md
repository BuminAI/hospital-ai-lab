When a vendor says its tool "makes the call," the next question from a nurse leader is usually, "Who can overrule it?" A new national survey suggests patients ask the same thing. The West Health-Gallup Center on Healthcare in America published a report on November 9, 2026, on how comfortable U.S. patients are with AI in their care, and how well physicians guess that comfort. Here is what it found, in plain terms, and what hospital administrators and nursing leaders can take from it.

## What was surveyed

Gallup surveyed 5,330 U.S. adults who had seen a primary care provider in the past year, using its web panel from August 12 to 24, 2026. The margin of sampling error is plus or minus 1.7 percentage points. A separate opt-in web survey reached 1,007 licensed, practicing primary care physicians from August 7 to 23, 2026. Because that physician survey is not a probability sample, its numbers describe the people who answered, not all physicians.

## The key number: who can say no

Gallup asked patients how comfortable they would be if AI made decisions, with and without a provider able to override them:

| Use of AI | Provider can override | No provider review |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Diagnosis and treatment | 56% | 9% |
| Imaging review | 53% | 13% |
| Medication decisions | 49% | 11% |

When AI only assists and the provider decides, the report gives 38% for diagnosis and treatment, 52% for imaging review, and 45% for medication decisions. In other words, comfort is tied less to how smart the tool is and more to whether a person stays in charge.

## Physicians guessed too low

Physicians underestimated patient comfort in the "provider can override" setting. For diagnosis and treatment the gap was 20 points. For imaging review, physicians estimated 42% against 53% for patients, and for medication decisions 42% against 49%. When AI only assists, physician estimates were within two points of patients for diagnosis and medication, though they were 11 points low on imaging review.

Asked what would make them more comfortable with AI acting on its own, no single factor won a majority. The most helpful was the ability to request provider review, chosen by just under half. Privacy protections, evidence of safety and effectiveness, lower costs and faster decisions each drew no more than a third.

## What hospital teams can do with this

Gallup's own suggestions are to ask patients directly about their preferences and to find out what training and support would help providers use AI well. It also reports that 89% of providers had used AI for at least one work task in the past year, while only 20% had received formal AI training for health care. The steps below are our suggestions, not Gallup's.

1. **Write the override into the workflow.** For any AI that suggests or flags something, state who reviews it, how they can overrule it, and where that is recorded. Nurses should know the path before the first alert.
2. **Make the review request easy.** If patients value being able to ask for a provider's review, tell front-line staff how to say so and how to route the request.
3. **Ask patients.** A short question at intake or in a patient advisory group can show how your community feels, instead of relying on staff guesses.
4. **Plan training.** Pair each new tool with a short training plan, since formal training is rare in the survey.

## Key takeaways

- Gallup (November 9, 2026): 56% of patients are comfortable with AI diagnosis and treatment if a provider can override it; 9% if no provider reviews it.
- Physicians underestimated patient comfort by 20 points in the override setting, and the physician survey is an opt-in sample.
- Spell out who can override, how requests for review are handled, and what training staff receive. This is general information, not legal advice.
