Hospital AI marketing often starts small: a chatbot drafts a newsletter, or an image tool makes a campaign picture. The tool is new, but the rules on patient information and advertising claims still apply. Check these four things before anything goes out.

In this article, "medical AI" means the broad field including tools that support care, "hospital AI" means AI used in hospital work, and "AI marketing" means using AI for promotion and patient outreach. These are working labels, not legal categories.

## 1. Is the message "marketing" under HIPAA?

Under [45 CFR 164.501](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-45/section-164.501), marketing means a communication about a product or service that encourages recipients to purchase or use it. The definition has exceptions, including certain treatment and health care operations communications, with conditions about payment from third parties. Have your privacy officer decide which side a campaign falls on before the list is pulled.

## 2. Do you have an authorization for the data you use?

[45 CFR 164.508(a)(3)](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-45/section-164.508) says a covered entity must obtain an authorization for any use or disclosure of protected health information for marketing, except a face-to-face communication to the individual or a promotional gift of nominal value. If a third party pays the covered entity for the marketing, the authorization must say so.

Pasting a patient list into an AI tool to segment or personalise a campaign is a use of that information. Settle the authorization question, and your vendor terms, first.

## 3. Is every testimonial real?

The FTC's rule at [16 CFR 465.2](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-16/section-465.2) addresses consumer testimonials that materially misrepresent that the person giving them exists, used the service, or had that experience. An AI-written "patient story" about someone who does not exist is exactly what to keep out of a campaign.

## 4. Are paid or insider endorsements disclosed?

[16 CFR 255.5](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-16/section-255.5) says that a connection between an endorser and the seller that might affect the weight of the endorsement, and that the audience would not expect, must be disclosed clearly and conspicuously. That can include payment, free products, or business and family relationships.

Whether the FTC's rules reach your organization depends on its legal form, and state law may add requirements this article does not cover. Ask counsel.

## Try it this week

- Pick three recent campaigns and note, for each, whose data was used and on what basis.
- Write a one-line rule: no AI-generated patients, clinicians or testimonials.
- Add a human sign-off, with name and date, for any AI-drafted claim.
- Planning a new AI tool? Run it through the [adoption checklist](/en/checklist/).

## Key takeaways

- Hospital AI marketing is still subject to HIPAA: marketing uses of protected health information generally need an authorization.
- Do not publish testimonials from people who do not exist or did not have the experience described.
- Disclose material connections between endorsers and your organization clearly.
- This is general information, not legal advice. Confirm with your privacy officer and counsel.
