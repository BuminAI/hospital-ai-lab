Some AI tools do not make a diagnosis. They point out a case that may need a closer look and send it down a referral pathway. On October 30, 2026, the FDA published an order creating a device type for this kind of software in lung imaging. The rule itself is about what manufacturers must show. But the way the FDA describes the risks is a useful checklist for hospitals deciding how such a flag fits into daily work. This post is general information, not legal advice.

## What the FDA order says

The [Federal Register order](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2026/10/30/2026-22180/medical-devices-radiology-devices-classification-of-the-radiology-software-for-referral-of-findings) adds 21 CFR 892.2085, "Radiology software for referral of findings related to fibrotic lung disease," as a class II device with special controls. The order is effective October 30, 2026; the classification itself was applicable from January 12, 2024, when the FDA granted the original De Novo request.

According to the [full text](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/full_text/text/2026/10/30/2026-22180.txt), the device is a prescription image-processing tool. It analyzes CT images to suggest disease or a finding suggestive of disease, and its output is adjunctive information within a referral pathway that is part of the overall diagnostic assessment. In other words, it adds information; it is not the final word.

## The risks the FDA lists

The order names the main risks to health:

- **False positives**: a flag that leads to wrong or delayed management of a patient who has a different lung disease.
- **False negatives**: no flag, so a patient with the disease is managed late or incorrectly.
- **Misuse**: running the software on the wrong patients, or on images from incompatible scanners or settings.
- **Overreliance**: misreading the output or leaning on it too heavily.
- **Device failure**: results that are missing, delayed, or wrong.

Labeling must describe the clinical context and workflow, compatible imaging hardware and protocols, performance testing results, and a warning to use the device together with other clinical and diagnostic findings.

## Questions for your team before go-live

These are our suggestions, not FDA requirements. Confirm details with your radiology, compliance, and IT leads.

1. **Who receives the flag?** Name the role that sees it, and the backup on nights and weekends.
2. **What happens next, and by when?** A referral flag is only useful if someone schedules the follow-up. Agree who owns that step.
3. **Does our scanner setup match the labeling?** Ask the vendor to confirm compatible hardware and protocols in writing.
4. **What if the software is down?** Plan how staff will know results are missing or late.

## Key takeaways

- The FDA created 21 CFR 892.2085, a class II device type for CT software that suggests fibrotic lung disease findings for referral.
- Its output is adjunctive information in a referral pathway, not a stand-alone answer.
- Listed risks include false positives and negatives, misuse on incompatible images, overreliance, and device failure.
- Before go-live, decide who receives the flag, who owns follow-up, and what to do if the tool fails.
