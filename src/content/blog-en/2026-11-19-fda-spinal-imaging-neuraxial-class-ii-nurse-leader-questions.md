Some vendors now sell imaging aids that help clinicians find the right spot for a spinal needle. A Federal Register order published on October 30, 2026 shows what FDA expects from this kind of device. For administrators and nurse leaders in anesthesia and procedure areas, it is a handy example of how to read a vendor's "FDA" claim. This post is general information, not legal advice.

## What the order does

According to the [Federal Register order](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/full_text/html/2026/10/30/2026-22181.html) (effective October 30, 2026), FDA classified the "spinal imaging system for neuraxial procedures" as a class II device with special controls. It is codified at 21 CFR 868.1985. The regulation describes the device as a sensor and software system that assists in identifying the interspinous space (the gap between spinal bones) for neuraxial procedures. The order does not describe the device as using artificial intelligence, so a vendor's AI claim is something to ask about separately. Devices in this class still go through premarket notification, known as 510(k).

## The risks FDA names

The order lists risks to health. Inaccurate identification of the space could mean increased pain, bleeding, an unintended puncture of the membrane around the spinal cord, or injury to deeper structures. Improper use could also lengthen the procedure. Other listed risks are electrical shock or interference with other devices, tissue reaction, and infection.

## What the special controls require

The special controls include clinical and non-clinical performance testing, human factors testing (checking that users can operate the device from the directions for use alone), software verification, validation and hazard analysis, electrical and electromagnetic safety testing, biocompatibility, sterility where labeled, and labeling. The labeling must warn that the risks of neuraxial procedures remain when the device is used.

## Questions a team can ask a vendor

These questions are drawn from the order's controls and are not legal requirements for your hospital:

- What did the clinical testing measure: success rate, procedure time, adverse events? How was it compared with manual palpation or other methods?
- Was training based only on the directions for use, and did users in the testing look like our staff?
- If the software is updated, how will we be told, and who checks it before use?

## Key takeaways

- FDA classified spinal imaging systems for neuraxial procedures as class II with special controls (21 CFR 868.1985), effective October 30, 2026.
- The order describes a sensor and software system and does not say it uses AI.
- Nurse leaders and administrators can use the listed risks and controls as vendor questions. Confirm specifics with your compliance staff.
