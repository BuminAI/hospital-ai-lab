A patient writes to the hospital: "I want a copy of my data, and I want to know whether a computer made any decisions about me." As more hospitals bring in AI tools for triage, scheduling or risk flags, the second half of that sentence will come up more often. The Data Protection Commission (DPC) explains the right of access on its website. This post reads two of its pages for individuals. It is general information, not legal advice; your data protection officer (DPO) is the person to confirm how it applies to you.

## What the patient can ask for

According to the DPC's page on the right of access, a person can get confirmation of whether their data is being processed and a copy of it. They are also entitled to supplementary information, including the purposes of processing, the categories of data, the recipients, how long the data will be kept (or the criteria for deciding), and their other rights.

The list also includes the existence of automated decision-making, including profiling, and meaningful information about how such decisions are made. That is the line hospital AI teams should notice.

## How long the hospital has

The DPC's page on timing says the controller must act without undue delay and within one month of receiving the request. That period may be extended by two further months where necessary, taking into account the complexity and number of requests. If the hospital extends, it must tell the person within one month of receiving the request and give reasons for the delay.

On fees, the DPC says a person cannot be required to pay a fee for most access requests. A reasonable fee based on administrative costs is possible only where a request is manifestly unfounded or excessive, or for additional copies. The person may be asked for evidence of identity, to avoid giving information to the wrong person.

## What to prepare now

These are our suggestions, not DPC requirements:

1. **List the AI tools that touch patient records.** For each, note whether it only helps staff or whether it makes or shapes a decision about the patient.
2. **Write a plain description of each tool.** Someone in the records office should be able to explain, in two or three sentences, what it does and who checks its output.
3. **Ask your DPO where AI outputs are stored,** such as risk scores or draft notes, and whether they would fall within an access request.
4. **Check that your one-month clock starts on receipt,** whichever door the request comes through: post, email or the front desk.

## Key takeaways

- The DPC lists information on automated decision-making, including profiling, among the things a person can learn through an access request.
- The hospital must respond within one month of receipt, extendable by two further months where necessary, with the person told within the first month.
- Most requests are free. Prepare plain descriptions of AI tools now. This is general information, not legal advice.
