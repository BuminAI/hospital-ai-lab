On 29 July 2026 HIQA published Ireland’s first National Guidance for the Responsible and Safe Use of Artificial Intelligence in Health and Social Care Services. If you run or advise a hospital, it is the most practical Irish document on the subject so far. One caveat up front: we could not open the final PDF, so this post relies on HIQA’s press release and on the consultation draft from January 2026. Where a detail comes from the draft, we say so, because the final text may differ.

## What HIQA says it is

HIQA’s press release says the guidance was commissioned by the Department of Health and developed with the Department and the HSE. It is intended for all services providing health and social care in Ireland, and it is also designed to educate and empower people using services about what to expect when AI is used in their care. It is organised around HIQA’s four principles for person-centred care and support: accountability, a human rights-based approach, safety and wellbeing, and responsiveness. It builds on HIQA’s existing national standards. It is guidance, not a statute.

## Five things the draft suggests a service puts in place

The January 2026 draft gives examples under its accountability principle. They read like a project checklist:

1. **A list of authorised AI tools,** kept by the service and shared with staff.
2. **A named person** who compiles and maintains an inventory of AI tools in use.
3. **Expert input before first use:** legal and regulatory advice, including on ethical and cyber security impacts and EU AI Act requirements.
4. **Quality control and risk assessment:** assurance practices for AI tools, with risk assessments inside existing risk management procedures.
5. **Fundamental rights impact assessments** for high-risk tools, as the EU AI Act requires.

The draft adds that structures should be proportionate to the size and complexity of the service. A small clinic does not need a hospital’s committee.

## People stay in charge

According to the draft, AI tools should augment, not replace, human judgement and must sit inside a process with appropriate human oversight. It describes a human who oversees decisions in real time (“human in the loop”) or a system that can be overridden (“human on the loop”), and says staff should be able to override an output. Management is responsible for decisions on using and monitoring AI tools, staff for interpreting outputs, and clinical staff remain responsible for their clinical decisions.

## If you build it yourself

The draft also warns that medical device software, including AI-based software, may be developed by hospitals themselves. In that case it says the hospital would need to meet provider obligations under the EU AI Act and manufacturer obligations under the Medical Device Regulation. Ask your IT and clinical engineering teams whether anything in-house could fall under that.

## Key takeaways

- HIQA’s national AI guidance (29 July 2026) is for all Irish health and social care services and is built on four principles.
- The January 2026 draft suggests a list of AI tools, a named inventory owner, expert input, risk assessments and fundamental rights assessments for high-risk tools.
- People stay responsible and able to override. In-house software may make you a provider or manufacturer.
- This is general information, not legal advice. Read the final guidance and ask your compliance officer.
