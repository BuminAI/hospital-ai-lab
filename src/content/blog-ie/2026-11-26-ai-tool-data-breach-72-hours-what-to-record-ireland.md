A summary meant for one team goes to the wrong list. A staff member pastes patient details into a chatbot that was never approved. A supplier says there was “a problem” with your data. In each case, the person who notices first is usually on a ward or at a desk, not in the data protection office. What they do in the first hour matters.

## What the DPC says

The Data Protection Commission’s [breach notification page](https://www.dataprotection.ie/en/organisations/know-your-obligations/breach-notification) says that where a breach presents a risk to the affected individuals, organisations must notify within 72 hours of becoming aware of it.

It adds three points that are easy to miss:

- Where a breach is likely to result in a **high risk** to the people affected, they must also be informed without undue delay.
- Even where you determine there is **no risk** to them, you need to keep an internal record of the details.
- If a notification was incomplete, further information should be submitted when it becomes available.

So the first report does not have to wait until every question is answered. Whether a particular event is a breach, and what level of risk it carries, is a judgement for your data protection officer, not for the person who spotted it.

## Where suppliers come in

The DPC’s page on [controller and processor relationships](https://www.dataprotection.ie/en/organisations/know-your-obligations/controller-and-processor-relationships) lists, among the terms a contract must contain, that the processor assists the controller in meeting its obligations, including those on breach notification. Read your AI supplier’s contract and find out who they tell, how, and how quickly. A clock that you only hear about late is a short clock.

## A one-page sheet for the unit

These are our suggestions, not DPC requirements.

1. **Call this person** (with an out-of-hours number and a deputy).
2. **Write down** when you noticed, what happened, which data, roughly how many people, and what you have done.
3. **Do only the containment steps you are authorised to do**, such as asking a wrong recipient to delete a message.
4. **Do not investigate alone** or delete logs to tidy up.
5. **Report near misses too**, so the team can fix the cause.

## Try it this week

Run a ten-minute exercise with a night-shift nurse and an administrator: an AI-written discharge summary has been emailed to the wrong patient. Ask who they call and what they write down. If the answers differ, update the sheet. Make clear that early reporting will not be met with blame.

## Key takeaways

- Where there is a risk to individuals, the DPC page sets 72 hours from awareness.
- High risk also means informing those affected without undue delay.
- Keep a record even when you conclude there is no risk.
- Check what your supplier contract says about breaches.
- This is general information, not legal advice. Confirm with your data protection officer.
