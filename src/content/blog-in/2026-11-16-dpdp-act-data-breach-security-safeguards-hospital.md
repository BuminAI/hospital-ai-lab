Picture the call: an AI vendor tells the hospital that a test server holding patient records was open to the internet over the weekend. The first questions are practical. What counts as a "breach"? Who must be told, and by when? India's Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023 answers some of this and leaves the rest to rules. This post reads only the Act itself, as published in the Gazette of India, and says plainly what it does not settle.

## What counts as a breach

Section 2(u) defines a "personal data breach" as any unauthorised processing of personal data, or accidental disclosure, acquisition, sharing, use, alteration, destruction or loss of access to it, that compromises its confidentiality, integrity or availability. Notice that it covers more than theft. Records that become unavailable, or that are changed without authority, are also within the definition.

## The duty to keep data safe

Section 8(5) says a data fiduciary (the body that decides why and how personal data is processed, which includes a hospital) must protect personal data in its possession or under its control by taking reasonable security safeguards to prevent a personal data breach. The same sentence covers processing done on its behalf by a data processor. In plain terms, using a vendor does not move the responsibility away from the hospital.

Section 8(4) adds that the fiduciary must put in place appropriate technical and organisational measures so that the Act and its rules are actually followed.

## The duty to tell

Section 8(6) says that if a breach happens, the data fiduciary must give the Data Protection Board and each affected data principal (the patient) an intimation of the breach, in the form and manner prescribed. The Act itself does not give a deadline or a format. Those are left to rules that this site has not read, so we cannot say how many hours or days apply. Do not copy a number from another country's law, because it may not match.

## What the Schedule says about penalties

The Schedule lists a penalty that may extend to two hundred and fifty crore rupees for breaching the obligation to take reasonable security safeguards (section 8(5)). It lists a penalty that may extend to two hundred crore rupees for failing to give the Board or affected patients notice of a breach (section 8(6)). The Schedule words both as amounts that "may extend" to those figures, so they are upper limits, not fixed amounts. Nothing here predicts what a hospital would face.

## What a hospital can prepare

The points below are practical suggestions, not legal advice.

1. **Put breach alerts in the vendor contract.** Ask for a written duty to tell the hospital quickly and to share what happened, since the hospital must inform the Board and patients.
2. **Name one person who decides.** Decide in advance who confirms that an event is a breach under the definition and who prepares the intimation.
3. **Keep a log.** Note when the hospital learned of the event, what data was involved and what was done, so the hospital can explain its steps later.
4. **Watch for the rules.** The form, manner and timing of the intimation come from rules, so check the current rules with your legal adviser before finalising any procedure.

## Key takeaways

- The Act defines a personal data breach widely (section 2(u)) and makes the hospital responsible for safeguards even when a processor handles the data (section 8(5)).
- After a breach, the hospital must give the Data Protection Board and each affected patient an intimation in the prescribed form and manner (section 8(6)); the Act itself sets no deadline.
- The Schedule sets maximum penalties of two hundred and fifty crore rupees for safeguards and two hundred crore rupees for notice failures. This is general information, not legal advice.
