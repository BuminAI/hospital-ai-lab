Many hospital forms already ask a patient to “consent” to the use of their data. The Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023 sets out in detail what has to come before that consent and what makes it valid. This note reads sections 5 and 6 for people who run a hospital. When each provision starts to apply depends on government notification, which this note comes back to at the end.

## The notice comes first

Under section 5(1), every request for consent must be accompanied or preceded by a notice. The notice must tell the person the personal data and the purpose for which it is to be processed, how they can exercise their rights, and how they can make a complaint to the Data Protection Board. Section 5(3) gives the person the option to read the notice in English or any language in the Eighth Schedule to the Constitution.

## What valid consent looks like

Section 6(1) says consent must be free, specific, informed, unconditional and unambiguous, with a clear affirmative action. It must signify agreement to processing for the specified purpose and be limited to the personal data necessary for that purpose. The Act gives an example from health: a telemedicine app that asks for consent to process data for telemedicine and also to access the phone’s contact list. The contact list is not necessary for telemedicine, so the consent is limited to what telemedicine needs.

Requests must be in clear and plain language, in English or an Eighth Schedule language, with the contact details of a Data Protection Officer where applicable or of another authorised person (section 6(3)).

## Withdrawal and proof

The person can withdraw consent at any time, and withdrawing must be as easy as giving it (section 6(4)). The hospital must then stop processing, and have its processors stop, within a reasonable time unless processing is required or authorised by law (section 6(6)). If a question arises, the data fiduciary has to prove that notice was given and consent obtained in line with the Act (section 6(10)).

## When consent is not the basis

Section 7 lists legitimate uses that do not depend on consent. They include responding to a medical emergency involving a threat to life or an immediate threat to health, and providing treatment or health services during an epidemic or outbreak. A routine form is not an emergency.

## Check the dates

Section 1(2) says the Act comes into force on a date the Central Government appoints by notification, and different dates may be appointed for different provisions. This site could not open the Ministry’s notifications and rules, so it does not state any date. Check the current notifications before you plan around one.

## Key takeaways

- A notice must come with or before each consent request (section 5), in English or an Eighth Schedule language.
- Consent must be free, specific, informed, unconditional and unambiguous, and limited to what the purpose needs (section 6).
- Withdrawal must be as easy as giving consent, and the hospital must be able to prove notice and consent.
- Medical emergencies and similar cases are legitimate uses without consent (section 7).
